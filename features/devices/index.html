On each device

Jarvis is one brain and four bodies. The brain is a Python process on a Raspberry Pi at home. It holds the memory, the event log, the model calls and the tools. The iPhone, Apple Watch, iPad and Mac reach it only over Tailscale (ADR 0170).

Each body keeps its own microphone, wake word, echo cancellation and speaker, because audio has to be handled where it is heard. The brain keeps the provider keys and the turn itself. That split is why a new device is a new body, not a new copy of Jarvis. Say “Hey Jarvis” with several devices around and exactly one answers, as Talking to it explains.

iPhone

Section titled “iPhone”

The phone has three pages: Today, Chat and Map. It senses most of your life too: location, motion, Health, and the apps in front. Press the Action button, say “Hey Jarvis” with the app open, or share anything to it from another app.

Apple Watch

Section titled “Apple Watch”

Tap to talk, and read the answer on your wrist. Reminders, “time to leave” and bus progress arrive as cards. The watch has no token and no line of its own. It talks to the phone, and the phone talks to the brain, so a turn that started on the watch is marked as one and answered in text. Without its phone, the watch shows the last day it was given.

iPad

Section titled “iPad”

The same day and chat on a bigger screen, plus Jarvis Remote: your Mac’s screen on the iPad at up to 120 fps, driven by touch, trackpad gestures and voice. A small Mac app of its own captures the display with ScreenCaptureKit and encodes it with VideoToolbox as HEVC. It takes connections only from the tailnet and the Mac itself, behind a pairing token, and it does not go through the brain.

The iPad senses nothing itself. On a non-phone device the app starts no collector and records nothing, so what Jarvis knows about your day comes from the devices that are with you.

Mac

Section titled “Mac”

On the Mac, Jarvis is a small glass ball with eyes beside the notch. It is built to be glanceable: nothing to open, nothing sitting over your work.

  • It is alive on purpose. It blinks, follows your pointer, and dozes after about ten minutes without you moving it. There is only one of it: when your pointer settles on another display, it moves there. On a screen with no notch it sits in a black pill at top centre.
  • It has skins. Hold it a moment and it changes its look.
  • A tap or a double-tap on ⌘ is the same thing. It starts a voice conversation, interrupts it mid-sentence, or ends it.
  • It shows what needs you. Your coding agents (Claude Code and Codex sessions) or the day’s timeline show beside the notch. When an agent wants to run a command, a card drops from the notch and you allow or deny it there.
  • It records screen activity for the day’s record, once you grant the permission.

It would rather stay quiet

Section titled “It would rather stay quiet”

A card at the notch is the only thing that can interrupt you, and every card has a small row asking whether its level was right. Your answer is kept on your machine with a snapshot of the card (its kind, title and the quiet level and hour), never what an agent wrote or a command. For now ratings are recorded, not learned from: the rules that set a card’s level are fixed, and the log lets a better rule be tested against them.

Quiet is one level held by the brain, so the notch, the sounds and the voice agree, and it survives a restart. Each level includes the one before:

  • Quiet: it never speaks unprompted and every cue sound is off. Cards still show.
  • No pop-ups: also no cards.
  • Do not disturb: also no visible reaction to anything outside.

Nothing held back is dropped. When you leave a quiet level, what waited arrives as one card, “N things while you were away”, with what needs you first. Reminders you set yourself ring at every level.
