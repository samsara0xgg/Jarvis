Look back on any day

Jarvis keeps your days from the signals your devices send. This page covers the part you look at: the Today page, the Map page, asking about a day in your own words, and the topics that let you pick a conversation up weeks later.

Today: one line of the day

Section titled “Today: one line of the day”

The Today page draws your waking day as one line, cut into stretches. Each stretch has a few words on what you were doing, like Writing the lab report or Bus 27 to campus. Open a stretch and it holds what happened inside it: pictures you sent, payments, calendar events, reminders, talks with Jarvis, coding sessions.

The program does the cutting and Claude does the wording. Code cuts the day where the facts change (a stay starts, the Mac goes idle, a walk begins) and gathers the clues for each stretch. Opus then writes, for every stretch, what you were doing and how sure it is. It labels the basis as observed, stated or inferred. A stretch that nothing speaks to stays marked unknown instead of being filled with a guess.

When it writes

Section titled “When it writes”

Not on a clock. Today’s line is written when you ask for it, and once more after the day is over. Until then, the stretches show their facts and no words. A whole day costs about 14 cents to write with Opus. A draft every two hours would have cost 25 to 35 dollars a month, so that mode exists behind a setting and ships off.

The final is written once per finished day, after the phone’s late data is in or twelve hours after you go to bed, whichever comes first. It is not rewritten when more rows trickle in, because a page that changes under you in the morning is worse than one that waits.

Your word wins

Section titled “Your word wins”

When Jarvis is unsure about a stretch, the stretch carries a question, for example whether it was a break. You answer there, or you correct the wording. Your answer is stored apart from the model’s runs and attaches by time, so it survives the next rewrite and shows with the basis stated. It also becomes a clue the next time you are at the same place. A day holds at most five questions, and nothing interrupts you during the day to ask them. They are also put to you in an evening review.

You can also mark a day’s highlights yourself by tapping a row. A day has at most five. A sixth is refused, and nothing you marked is dropped to make room.

A day ends when you go to bed

Section titled “A day ends when you go to bed”

A day runs until your night’s sleep begins, even past midnight, and the next day’s page shows that tail as well. Cutting at 00:00 would split one evening at the desk into two spans on two days, worded by two runs that never see each other.

Map: where you went

Section titled “Map: where you went”

The Map page replays your outings. An outing is the time between two long stays. It has a route, the stops along the way, your speed along the route, the distance, and your heart rate from the Apple Watch set against your usual rate at home while awake.

All of it comes from the iPhone, which sends its GPS fixes and its motion (walking, riding, driving, still) in batches over HTTP, because iOS only wakes an app for a few seconds at a time. The brain turns them into stays, walks, rides and drives when the page is read, and stores no timeline of its own, so a late batch simply changes the answer.

Stays are cut from the fixes themselves. iOS’s own visit detection reported visits on only 3 of 9 days, while fixes arrived on every day that included going out. A stay is a run of accurate fixes within 150 m of each other lasting at least 5 minutes. The phone stops sending when you stop moving, so a long gap at one spot means you stayed there, not that data is missing.

Ask about it

Section titled “Ask about it”

Ask in your own words: how much did I walk today?, what did I tell Mia about the trip?, when did I get home on Tuesday? For a day, Jarvis reads that day’s rows, up to 20 of them, each with an id. The answer then names the rows it is about, at most three, and they appear as links: tap one and the Today page opens at that row, or the Map opens on that trip.

The program keeps only links to rows that Jarvis was shown in that turn. An id it wrote without having read it is dropped without a word, because a link the phone opens as fact must be one that was actually read.

Topics: pick a thread up later

Section titled “Topics: pick a thread up later”

The chat is one continuous conversation. On top of it, Jarvis keeps topics: a person, a plan, a project, an event. A topic has a name, whether it is open or done, one line on where it stands, and a short recap. Go into a topic and Jarvis starts from the recap, not from every earlier line. You rename, merge or close a topic by telling it.

Each night, Opus decides which topics the day talked about and rewrites their line and recap from the day’s raw words. It does not file individual lines. The first design did, and it filed loosely: greetings and weather answers ended up under unrelated matters, and one topic had gathered 380 lines. A topic keeps only its core. When you need an earlier detail, Jarvis searches the conversation itself.

The morning brief of yesterday

Section titled “The morning brief of yesterday”

A separate, older feature still runs. Once a day, at 05:00 by default (the daily_report setting), Jarvis writes a report of the day before. Claude writes it, at high effort, from that day’s conversations, git commits, your calendar and to-dos, and screen activity if you switched that on. Codex sessions are read only if you turn that on as well. It runs on a schedule and never on request: when it ran on demand, a spoken question set off a run of 59 seconds and about 59 cents, with nothing said meanwhile.

It is built not to overstate. Claims with no citation are thrown out, a commit counts only as committed, and a second pass reads the cited originals before anything is called done.
